<a id="advanced-return-pressure"></a>
## Venous-return curves, transmural pressure and feedback timing

- Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology (2026).pdf — PDF pp. 233-263

At a steady operating point cardiac output equals venous return. A cardiac-function curve and a venous-return curve must be solved together. Mean systemic filling pressure is the zero-flow intercept of the return relation; resistance to venous return determines its slope. Increasing resistance flattens the curve without necessarily moving the intercept. Increasing stressed volume or venous tone usually raises the intercept. At very negative right-atrial pressure, collapse of compliant veins creates a plateau, so extrapolating the straight line predicts an impossible unlimited return.
Transmural filling pressure is intracavitary pressure minus surrounding pressure. Raising pressure around the heart by 6 mmHg requires a 6-mmHg rise inside to preserve the same distending pressure. Thus positive-pressure ventilation can raise measured central venous pressure while reducing effective filling and venous return. These effects depend on lung mechanics, blood volume and ventricular function; CVP alone is not a direct measure of preload volume.

<a id="arterial-pressure"></a>
## Compliance, pulse pressure, MAP and measurement

- Cairculatory System 3.pptx — Slides 8-17
- Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology (2026).pdf — PDF pp. 187-196

Compliance is ΔV/ΔP. Distensibility is fractional volume change per pressure change, ΔV/(VΔP), so compliance equals distensibility times initial volume. Veins are highly compliant volume reservoirs. Arterial elastic recoil sustains diastolic flow after ventricular ejection. Pulse pressure is systolic minus diastolic pressure and approximately rises with stroke volume and falls with arterial compliance, although ejection speed and wave reflection also matter.
For a resting rate, MAP is often estimated as DBP plus one-third pulse pressure. For 120/80 mmHg this is about 93 mmHg, not 100 exactly. The arithmetic average is wrong because diastole lasts longer. At high heart rates or unusual waveforms, the approximation is less accurate and waveform integration is preferable. Both systolic and diastolic pressures reflect multiple cardiac and vascular factors; they are not isolated tests of heart health versus vessel health.

<a id="atrial-septation"></a>
## Atrial septation, sinus venosus and pulmonary veins

- Langman's Medical Embryology 15th Edition - OCR.pdf — PDF pp. 200-207
- Cardiovascular - Heart formation — Transcript EN.md — Atrial and venous-incorporation segments

Septum primum descends from the common atrial roof toward endocardial cushions, leaving ostium primum below. Before this lower opening closes, perforations coalesce in the upper primum to create ostium secundum. Septum secundum develops to the right and leaves the foramen ovale. The remaining primum serves as a flap valve. Blood can pass right-to-left before birth while the offset septa prevent a simple straight unobstructed hole after pressure reversal.
After birth increased pulmonary return raises LA pressure relative to RA, pressing primum against secundum for functional closure. Anatomical fusion is later and may be incomplete. A patent foramen ovale is failure of fusion of an otherwise formed flap, whereas a true septal defect involves deficient tissue. Excessive resorption of primum or inadequate secundum development can cause a secundum ASD. A primum defect is associated with endocardial cushion/AV septal abnormalities.

<a id="blood-groups"></a>
## ABO, Rh and transfusion concepts

- Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology (2026).pdf — PDF pp. 474-479

ABO antigens are carbohydrate determinants on RBC surfaces. Plasma commonly contains antibodies against the absent A/B antigen, with important age and immune-history exceptions. A recipient receiving incompatible donor RBCs can develop antibody-mediated agglutination and hemolysis. RBC and plasma compatibility run in different directions because one product supplies cells and the other supplies antibodies.
Rh D is a major RBC protein antigen. Anti-D generally follows exposure rather than being naturally present in an unsensitized person. Maternal IgG can cross the placenta and damage fetal RBCs if fetal antigen is incompatible with maternal sensitization. Distinguish immune hemolysis from an intrinsic membrane or hemoglobin defect. The phrase universal donor is context-dependent: component type, Rh, minor antigens and crossmatching remain important.

<a id="cardiac-ap"></a>
## Cardiac action potentials and excitation-contraction coupling

- Cardiac action potential with naration.ppt — Slides 1-17
- Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology (2026).pdf — PDF pp. 121-125, 135-141

Depolarization travels down T tubules, opens L-type calcium channels and triggers ryanodine receptor-mediated calcium release from sarcoplasmic reticulum. Calcium binds troponin C, permitting actin-myosin cycling. Relaxation requires SERCA reuptake, sodium-calcium exchange and extrusion. ATP is required both for cross-bridge cycling and restoration of calcium gradients. The long action potential/refractory period prevents fused tetanic contraction and preserves filling intervals.
Beta-1 stimulation increases cAMP-dependent calcium handling and pacemaker activity, raising contractility and rate while accelerating relaxation. Parasympathetic M2 signaling reduces cAMP and increases potassium conductance, slowing nodal firing/conduction. Do not repeat ASR phrases that swap sympathetic with parasympathetic or call outward K+ current an influx.

<a id="cardiac-nerves"></a>
## Autonomic cardiac and mediastinal nerves

- Heart 4.pptx — Slide 4
- Mediastinum 2.pptx — Slide 3
- Mediastinum 3.pptx — Slides 3-6
- Greys Anatomy - Anatomical Basis of Clinical Practice 42nd Edition.pdf — PDF pp. 1633-1636, 1715-1717

Sympathetic preganglionic neurons arise in upper thoracic cord segments and synapse in cervical/upper thoracic sympathetic ganglia. Cardiac postganglionic nerves reach the superficial and deep cardiac plexuses. Their norepinephrine acts mainly through beta-1 receptors to increase rate, conduction and contractility. Vagal preganglionic fibers synapse near/in the heart and decrease SA firing and AV conduction, with weaker direct ventricular effects. Intrinsic cardiac ganglia and nerve plexuses are not the SA/AV nodes: the latter are specialized myocytes.
Pain afferents from the heart usually travel with sympathetic pathways to upper thoracic dorsal root ganglia, explaining referred chest/medial-arm patterns. Reflex afferents travel largely with vagus. Phrenic pain from parietal pericardium refers to cervical/shoulder territory instead. These three sensory pathways should be compared explicitly in difficult MCQs.

<a id="coagulation"></a>
## Coagulation cascade, anticoagulants and fibrinolysis

- blood 2.ppsx — Slides 15-31
- Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology (2026).pdf — PDF pp. 483-491

Tissue factor with factor VIIa initiates coagulation after injury. The classic intrinsic scheme uses XII, XI, IX and VIII; the extrinsic route is tissue factor/VII. They converge on factor X. Xa with Va, calcium and a phospholipid surface forms prothrombinase, generating thrombin from prothrombin. Thrombin converts fibrinogen to fibrin, activates factor XIII to cross-link it and amplifies activation of platelets and several cofactors. The cell-based in-vivo process is more integrated than two independent linear tubes.
Serum is the liquid remaining after clotting and differs from plasma by consumption/removal of fibrinogen and changes in several coagulation proteins. Most clotting proteins are synthesized in liver, but the slide statement that all except tissue factor come from hepatocytes is too broad: endothelial cells are important for vWF and factor VIII. Vitamin K supports gamma-carboxylation needed for calcium-dependent membrane binding. Fibrinolysis is regulated continuously and should not be imagined as beginning only a fixed number of days after injury.

<a id="conduction-phys"></a>
## Pacemakers, AV delay and conduction failure

- Rythmical Excitation.ppt — Slides 1-5
- Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology (2026).pdf — PDF pp. 135-141

The SA node normally dominates because its spontaneous depolarization reaches threshold faster than other latent pacemakers. Impulses spread through atria, slow in AV nodal tissue, then traverse His bundle, bundle branches and Purkinje network to activate ventricular myocardium rapidly. AV delay permits atrial contraction to precede ventricular contraction. Purkinje conduction is rapid; AV nodal conduction is relatively slow. The fibrous AV separation forces the normal impulse through this specialized connection.
An ectopic focus or re-entry circuit can compete with the sinus node. Re-entry requires an excitable pathway, unidirectional block and a conduction delay sufficient for tissue ahead to recover excitability. A premature ventricular beat can produce a wide QRS because activation spreads abnormally through ventricular myocardium rather than rapidly through the normal network. AV nodal blockade can expose a slower escape rhythm from subsidiary pacemakers.

<a id="coronary"></a>
## Coronary arterial territories and venous drainage

- Greys Anatomy - Anatomical Basis of Clinical Practice 42nd Edition.pdf — PDF pp. 1699-1715
- Heart 4.pptx — Slides 2-4

Dominance refers principally to the artery supplying the inferior/posterior interventricular branch, not the largest artery or the side with the thickest ventricle. The RCA is commonly dominant, but reported proportions differ by definition and population. The LAD usually supplies anterior two-thirds of the interventricular septum and the PDA posterior/inferior third, with overlap and variation. A proximal LAD lesion can impair conduction and large LV territory, whereas an inferior RCA event may affect AV conduction if the nodal branch is involved.
The great cardiac vein accompanies LAD and continues toward the coronary sinus. The middle cardiac vein follows the inferior interventricular groove. The small cardiac vein follows the right margin/AV groove. The coronary sinus lies posteriorly in the AV groove and opens into RA. Anterior cardiac veins drain RV directly into RA, and smallest cardiac veins empty directly into chambers. Thus not all venous blood from myocardium passes through the coronary sinus.

<a id="coronary-exercise"></a>
## Coronary perfusion, exercise and oxygen demand

- Cairculatory System 6.pptx — Slides 4-14
- Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology (2026).pdf — PDF pp. 264-275

Left ventricular coronary flow is strongly reduced during systole by compression of intramyocardial vessels, especially in subendocardial layers, and is greatest during diastole. Right ventricular flow is less suppressed because RV systolic pressure is lower. Coronary perfusion pressure is often approximated by aortic diastolic pressure minus LV end-diastolic pressure. Tachycardia both increases oxygen demand and shortens diastolic perfusion time.
Myocardium extracts a high fraction of delivered oxygen at rest. Increased demand therefore requires increased coronary flow more than a large extraction reserve. Increased heart rate, contractility and wall stress raise oxygen consumption. With a thin-wall model, tension rises with pressure and radius, while increased wall thickness lowers stress. Dilated ventricles can require more oxygen for the same external work. This links Laplace reasoning to ischemia and failure.

<a id="cycle"></a>
## Cardiac cycle, pressure crossings and heart sounds

- Cardiac Physiology with naration 1.ppt — Slides 1-10
- Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology (2026).pdf — PDF pp. 126-130

Valve motion follows pressure gradients. At the start of ventricular systole, ventricular pressure exceeds atrial pressure and AV valves close. During isovolumetric contraction both inlet and outlet valves of that ventricle are closed, pressure rises and volume remains at EDV. When ventricular pressure exceeds arterial pressure, the semilunar valve opens and ejection begins. At the end of ejection, arterial pressure exceeds ventricular pressure and the semilunar valve closes. Isovolumetric relaxation reduces pressure at ESV until atrial pressure opens the AV valve.
P represents atrial depolarization, not atrial contraction itself. QRS precedes ventricular pressure rise, and T reflects ventricular repolarization. At faster rates diastole shortens disproportionately, reducing filling time and coronary perfusion time. Right and left heart valves do not all change state at exactly the same instant. Inspiration normally delays pulmonary closure relative to aortic closure, producing physiologic S2 splitting.

<a id="ecg-axis"></a>
## Cardiac axis and vector interpretation

- ECG  & vectorial analysis with narration - Copy — Readable copy.pptx — Slides 4-13
- Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology (2026).pdf — PDF pp. 150-164

The frontal QRS axis reflects the net ventricular depolarization vector. Lead I points to 0 degrees, II to +60, aVF to +90, III to +120, aVL to -30 and aVR to -150. An isoelectric/equiphasic QRS suggests the mean vector is approximately perpendicular to that lead. There are always two opposite perpendicular possibilities; polarity in another lead selects the correct one.
For an equiphasic aVR, candidate directions are about -60 and +120 degrees. Positive lead I and negative inferior leads favor -60, while negative I and positive inferior leads favor +120. Do not infer one number from aVR alone. The common adult normal range is approximately -30 to +90 degrees, with variation by age, body habitus and reference. Positive I and negative aVF can still be normal if lead II is positive; this distinguishes a mildly leftward normal axis from more definite left-axis deviation.

<a id="ecg-foundations"></a>
## ECG calibration, lead geometry and a systematic interpretation

- ECG Eng narration.ppt — Slides 1-64
- ECG  & vectorial analysis with narration - Copy — Readable copy.pptx — Slides 1-13
- Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology (2026).pdf — PDF pp. 142-164

An ECG records differences in extracellular electrical potential. A depolarization wave moving toward a positive electrode produces an upward deflection; repolarization has opposite electrical polarity. A lead is a viewing axis and is not identical to an electrode. Twelve conventional leads use ten electrodes. Limb lead I is LA-RA, II is LL-RA and III is LL-LA, so II = I + III. Augmented leads and precordial leads provide additional views.
Before diagnosing, confirm patient/context, artifact, speed, gain and lead placement. Then assess rate, regularity, P-wave behavior, P-QRS relationship, PR, QRS width, axis, R-wave progression and ST-T changes. At 25 mm/s a regular rate is 300 divided by large squares between R waves or 1500 divided by small squares. For an irregular rhythm, count QRS complexes in a known time interval and scale to one minute. A short snapshot can misrepresent variability.

<a id="erythropoiesis"></a>
## Erythropoiesis, iron and anemia mechanisms

- blood 1.ppsx — Slides 8-32
- Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology (2026).pdf — PDF pp. 440-449
- Junqueira's Basic Histology 16th Edition.pdf — PDF pp. 265-274

Low tissue oxygen availability increases renal erythropoietin production through hypoxia-responsive signaling. EPO promotes erythroid progenitor survival and differentiation in marrow. The series proceeds through proerythroblast, basophilic, polychromatophilic and orthochromatic erythroblast stages, nuclear extrusion, reticulocyte and mature RBC. Cell size and nuclear size fall while hemoglobin accumulates. Reticulocytes retain residual RNA and indicate recent marrow output, but the percentage must be interpreted relative to the anemia and maturation time.
Vitamin B12 and folate support DNA synthesis, so deficiency produces ineffective, megaloblastic erythropoiesis. Iron deficiency limits heme synthesis and commonly produces microcytic hypochromic cells. Chronic blood loss can deplete iron, while acute blood loss is initially normocytic and may not immediately lower concentration before fluid redistribution. Renal failure can reduce EPO, marrow failure can impair multiple cell lines, and hemolysis increases red-cell destruction.

<a id="flow-laws"></a>
## Pressure, flow, resistance and vascular networks

- Cairculatory System 1.pptx — Slides 16-28
- Cairculatory System 2.pptx — Slides 5-10
- Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology (2026).pdf — PDF pp. 177-186

Flow Q equals pressure difference divided by resistance: Q = (Pin - Pout)/R. A high absolute pressure does not imply high flow if downstream pressure rises equally. For systemic circulation, the driving difference is approximately MAP minus right atrial pressure. Pulmonary resistance uses mean pulmonary arterial minus left atrial/wedge pressure, divided by pulmonary flow. Always keep units consistent: pressure in mmHg and flow in L/min yield mmHg·min/L, not the older mmHg·s/mL resistance unit.
For an ideal rigid cylindrical tube with steady laminar Newtonian flow, resistance is 8ηL/(πr^4). Doubling radius reduces resistance to one-sixteenth if other factors remain unchanged. Halving radius raises resistance sixteenfold. Real blood is non-Newtonian and vessels are compliant/branching/pulsatile, so this law is a model rather than an exact description of every vessel. Hematocrit strongly affects viscosity. Plasma proteins also contribute. Values depend on temperature and shear rate.

<a id="heart-external"></a>
## Heart surfaces, borders, grooves and imaging silhouette

- Heart 2.pptx — Slides 1-10
- Greys Anatomy - Anatomical Basis of Clinical Practice 42nd Edition.pdf — PDF pp. 1666-1672

The coronary sulcus separates atria from ventricles externally and carries coronary vessels. The anterior interventricular sulcus contains the anterior interventricular artery and great cardiac vein. The posterior/inferior interventricular sulcus contains the posterior/inferior interventricular artery and middle cardiac vein. The crux is the posterior junction of atrioventricular and interventricular grooves. A groove marks a boundary but its contents and arterial dominance can vary.
On a standard frontal chest radiograph the right contour is formed principally by SVC and right atrium. The left contour includes aortic knob, pulmonary trunk, left auricle and LV. The right ventricle is mainly anterior and usually does not create the main right silhouette. A radiographic border is therefore not a simple map of everything on that side of a cadaveric heart.

<a id="heart-fields"></a>
## Cardiogenic fields, folding and heart-tube derivatives

- Langman's Medical Embryology 15th Edition - OCR.pdf — PDF pp. 193-199
- Cardiovascular - Heart formation — Transcript EN.md — Initial cardiac field, folding and looping segments

Cardiac progenitors migrate through the primitive streak into splanchnic lateral plate mesoderm cranial to the neural folds. The primary heart field contributes especially to the left ventricle and parts of atria; the second heart field adds cells to the poles, particularly the RV and outflow tract. Left-right signaling during gastrulation is essential for correct looping and alignment. Cephalic and lateral folding bring cardiogenic tissue ventral and into the thorax and establish a continuous heart tube.
The primitive tube has endocardium, myocardium with intervening cardiac jelly, and later an epicardial covering derived from the proepicardial region. The dorsal mesocardium initially suspends it; breakdown centrally creates the transverse pericardial sinus. No normal ventral mesocardium is formed. Looping begins around day 23 and is largely complete by day 28. The cephalic ventricular/outflow region moves ventrally, caudally and rightward, while the atrial region moves dorsocranially and leftward.

<a id="immune-foundations"></a>
## Lymphocytes, antigen presentation and antibody responses

- Junqueira's Basic Histology 16th Edition.pdf — PDF pp. 278-287
- Immune System and Lymphatic Organs with narration.pptx — Slides 1-16

Innate responses use germline-encoded recognition and act rapidly; adaptive responses expand antigen-specific lymphocyte clones and generate memory. B cells recognize native antigen through surface immunoglobulin and can differentiate into antibody-secreting plasma cells. T cells recognize peptide-MHC complexes with costimulatory/contextual signals. CD4 helper T cells recognize MHC II, whereas CD8 cytotoxic T cells recognize MHC I. Most nucleated cells express MHC I; professional APCs provide MHC II presentation and activation signals.
Dendritic cells initiate many naive T-cell responses. Macrophages also present processed antigen and carry out phagocytosis. Follicular dendritic cells are stromal cells that display native antigen/immune complexes to B cells and are not the same as migratory antigen-processing dendritic cells. Germinal centers contain proliferating and selected B cells, while a surrounding mantle contains smaller resting B cells. Plasma cells have an eccentric nucleus, coarse chromatin and a pale Golgi region in basophilic cytoplasm.

<a id="local-regulation"></a>
## Local blood-flow control, autoregulation and special circuits

- Cairculatory System 4.pptx — Slides 16-17
- Cairculatory System 5.pptx — Slides 4-13
- Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology (2026).pdf — PDF pp. 210-221, 264-275

Active hyperemia is increased flow during increased tissue metabolism. Reactive hyperemia is transient excess flow after release of occlusion because vasodilator metabolites and oxygen debt accumulated during ischemia. Myogenic autoregulation responds to increased vessel stretch with contraction, stabilizing flow despite a changed perfusion pressure. Metabolic autoregulation links local flow to nutrient demand and waste accumulation. These mechanisms interact and have finite operating ranges.
Reduced local oxygen and increased CO2/H+, K+, adenosine and other metabolites generally dilate systemic tissue resistance vessels. Endothelial shear stress stimulates nitric oxide, which activates smooth-muscle cGMP and relaxation. Endothelin is a potent constrictor. Histamine and bradykinin can promote dilation and increased permeability. Epinephrine is receptor- and dose-dependent: beta-2 effects can dilate some beds while alpha effects constrict others. It must not be classified as universally vasoconstricting.

<a id="longterm-pressure"></a>
## Renal-body fluid feedback and hormonal pressure control

- Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology (2026).pdf — PDF pp. 233-249
- Cairculatory System 5.pptx — Slides 11, 19-21

Long-term pressure control depends strongly on sodium and water balance. Pressure natriuresis and diuresis increase salt/water excretion when renal perfusion pressure rises, reducing extracellular volume, venous return and ultimately output. The relation can shift with renal disease, sympathetic input and hormones. A single elevated reading does not establish a chronic set point or identify its mechanism.
Reduced renal perfusion, lower macula-densa salt delivery and beta-1 sympathetic drive stimulate renin. Renin cleaves angiotensinogen, and ACE generates angiotensin II. Angiotensin II constricts resistance vessels and stimulates aldosterone, thirst and vasopressin-related water conservation. Aldosterone increases distal sodium retention. Natriuretic peptides oppose volume expansion by promoting natriuresis and modulating renal/vascular function. Vasopressin increases collecting-duct water permeability and, at sufficiently high concentrations, can constrict vessels.

<a id="microexchange"></a>
## Microcirculation, Starling forces, lymph and edema

- Cairculatory System 4.pptx — Slides 4-11
- Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology (2026).pdf — PDF pp. 197-209
- Junqueira's Basic Histology 16th Edition.pdf — PDF pp. 237-244

Diffusion dominates exchange of gases and many small solutes. Lipid-soluble substances cross endothelial cell membranes readily, whereas hydrophilic solutes use clefts/fenestrae or transport pathways depending on the organ. Bulk fluid movement is governed by hydrostatic and oncotic forces plus filtration coefficient and barrier properties. It should not be confused with molecular diffusion.
The conventional Starling expression is Jv = Kf[(Pc - Pi) - σ(πc - πi)]. Capillary hydrostatic pressure promotes outward filtration, interstitial pressure opposes it when positive, plasma oncotic pressure opposes filtration and interstitial oncotic pressure favors it. The reflection coefficient describes restriction of protein movement. In glycocalyx-aware physiology, the effective oncotic gradient is across the endothelial surface layer and subglycocalyx space. Do not assume every systemic capillary routinely reabsorbs large amounts of fluid along its venous end; lymphatic return is essential.

<a id="neural-pressure"></a>
## Baroreceptors, chemoreceptors and central pressure regulation

- Cairculatory System 4.pptx — Slides 18-20
- Cairculatory System 5.pptx — Slides 14-21
- Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology (2026).pdf — PDF pp. 222-232

Carotid sinus and aortic arch baroreceptors sense stretch. Increased pressure increases afferent firing through Hering/glossopharyngeal and vagal pathways to the nucleus tractus solitarius. The integrated response decreases sympathetic tone and increases cardiac vagal activity, reducing rate, contractility and vascular resistance. A fall in pressure produces the opposite response. Rapid changes strongly influence firing. Baroreceptors buffer acute changes and can reset with sustained pressure changes.
Carotid and aortic bodies are peripheral chemoreceptors sensing oxygen, CO2 and H+. Their contribution to ventilation and circulation depends on the context, especially whether breathing is allowed to change. In a low-perfusion state they can contribute sympathetic vasoconstriction. The slide statement that a generic chemoreflex simply lowers pressure should not replace this integrated account. Carotid sinus is a pressure sensor and carotid body a chemical sensor despite their proximity.

<a id="pericardium"></a>
## Pericardial layers, sinuses and tamponade

- Heart 1.pptx — Slides 3-12
- Greys Anatomy - Anatomical Basis of Clinical Practice 42nd Edition.pdf — PDF pp. 1654-1666

The tough fibrous pericardium blends with the central tendon inferiorly and with great-vessel adventitia superiorly. Its inner surface carries parietal serous pericardium. Visceral serous pericardium is epicardium on the heart. The lubricated potential cavity is between the two serous layers. Coronary vessels and fat on the epicardial surface do not lie loose within the pericardial cavity.
The transverse sinus passes behind the ascending aorta and pulmonary trunk, in front of the SVC and superior to the left atrium. It allows passage behind the arterial outflow roots. The oblique sinus is a blind recess behind the left atrium bounded by serous reflections around pulmonary veins and IVC. A posterior cardiac surface and a pericardial sinus are different concepts. The anterior view can conceal the oblique sinus and posterior pulmonary veins.

<a id="pv-loop"></a>
## Pressure-volume loops, preload, afterload and contractility

- Cardiac physiology with naration 2.pptx — Slides 3-7
- Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology (2026).pdf — PDF pp. 129-134, 250-260

Stroke volume is EDV minus ESV, and ejection fraction is stroke volume divided by EDV. A normal teaching example of EDV 120 mL and ESV 50 mL gives SV 70 mL and EF about 58%. Cardiac output is HR times SV, so 70 beats/min times 70 mL is 4.9 L/min. These numbers are illustrative, not universal individual reference intervals. A PV loop is traced counterclockwise: filling, isovolumetric contraction, ejection and isovolumetric relaxation.
Preload describes initial myocardial stretch and is approximated by end-diastolic volume/pressure only in context. Afterload is wall stress opposing shortening, not simply blood pressure in every situation. Contractility is intrinsic force generation at a given loading condition, not increased force merely because preload rose. The loop area represents external stroke work. Pressure development and wall tension also consume oxygen even when ejected volume is limited.

<a id="rbc"></a>
## Erythrocytes, hematocrit and oxygen delivery

- blood 1.ppsx — Slides 2-17
- Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology (2026).pdf — PDF pp. 440-449
- Junqueira's Basic Histology 16th Edition.pdf — PDF pp. 248-255, 265-274

Mature erythrocytes are flexible biconcave anucleate cells approximately 7-8 micrometers across. Their shape increases surface area relative to volume and permits capillary passage. Hemoglobin carries oxygen, carbonic anhydrase accelerates CO2 hydration, and hemoglobin buffers H+. Lacking mitochondria, RBCs obtain ATP through glycolysis rather than consuming the oxygen they transport. Membrane-cytoskeletal integrity and redox protection are essential for their survival.
Hematocrit is the packed RBC fraction of whole blood volume. Hemoglobin concentration, RBC count and hematocrit are related but are not interchangeable. Anemia is principally a reduction in hemoglobin/oxygen-carrying capacity relative to the appropriate reference population, not necessarily a reduction in the number of cells. Plasma volume changes can alter measured concentration without an equivalent immediate change in total red-cell mass.

<a id="shock-failure"></a>
## Shock, heart failure and valve lesions: integrated physiology

- Cairculatory System 6.pptx — Slide 15
- Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology (2026).pdf — PDF pp. 276-307

Shock is inadequate effective tissue perfusion, not simply a low cuff pressure. Hypovolemic shock reduces circulating volume and preload. Cardiogenic shock reflects inadequate pump function. Obstructive shock impedes filling or outflow, as in tamponade or major pulmonary vascular obstruction. Distributive shock lowers effective vascular tone and can involve maldistributed flow. The early hemodynamic pattern depends on cause and stage.
Hemorrhage reduces systemic filling pressure and venous return. Baroreflex activation causes tachycardia, increased sympathetic contractility and vasoconstriction, with cold skin and reduced renal flow in a typical compensated pattern. Renin-angiotensin, vasopressin and fluid shifts contribute later. Do not memorize the lecture assertion that a fixed percentage of bleeding inevitably causes irreversible death or that contractility never increases. Outcome depends on rate, severity, compensation and treatment.

<a id="thoracic-map"></a>
## Thoracic boundaries, apertures and surface planes

- Bones 1.pptx — Slides 3-7
- Greys Anatomy - Anatomical Basis of Clinical Practice 42nd Edition.pdf — PDF pp. 1526-1536

The thorax is a truncated cone whose narrow superior aperture communicates with the neck. Its boundary is the body of T1 posteriorly, the first ribs and their cartilages laterally, and the superior border of the manubrium anteriorly. The aperture is oblique, sloping downward anteriorly. The inferior aperture is bounded by T12, ribs 11-12, the costal margin formed by cartilages 7-10, and the xiphisternal region. The diaphragm closes it, but the diaphragm domes project upward into the thorax.
The sternal angle is the palpable manubriosternal junction and the attachment level of the second costal cartilage. It is the reference for counting ribs and intercostal spaces. Its traditional transverse plane reaches the T4-T5 disc and separates superior from inferior mediastinum. The tracheal bifurcation, beginning/end of the aortic arch and azygos arch lie approximately near this level, with respiratory and positional variation. Surface levels are useful approximations, not rigid coordinates.

<a id="velocity-turbulence"></a>
## Velocity, Bernoulli, Reynolds and flow measurement

- Cairculatory System 2.pptx — Slides 10-16
- Cairculatory System 3.pptx — Slides 4, 7, 13-17
- Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology (2026).pdf — PDF pp. 178-184

Average velocity equals flow divided by total cross-sectional area, v = Q/A. Capillary velocity is low because the many parallel capillaries have a very large aggregate area. A small single vessel does not necessarily have slower velocity if the same flow is forced through it. Across a narrowed segment at fixed flow, velocity rises. By contrast, when pressure gradient is fixed and a tube narrows, resistance rises and flow falls. The held-constant condition determines the correct answer.
Reynolds number is proportional to density × velocity × diameter / viscosity. Higher Reynolds numbers favor turbulence. Rapid flow, large diameter and low viscosity can increase the tendency. A stenosis promotes disturbances through high local velocity and geometry despite its reduced diameter. The lecture inequalities on laminar/turbulent flow are reversed: turbulence is associated with higher, not lower, Reynolds number. Thresholds in pulsatile branching vessels are not one universal hard boundary.

<a id="venous-return"></a>
## Venous return, capacitance, posture and cardiac output

- Cairculatory System 4.pptx — Slides 12-15
- Cairculatory System 5.pptx — Slides 19-21
- Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology (2026).pdf — PDF pp. 250-263

At steady state cardiac output equals venous return. Venous return can be modeled as (mean systemic filling pressure - right atrial pressure)/resistance to venous return. Increasing right atrial pressure tends to oppose return if other variables are unchanged, while increasing systemic filling pressure favors return. Venoconstriction recruits blood from an unstressed reservoir into stressed volume and can raise systemic filling pressure. Infusion can also shift the venous return curve, whereas hemorrhage shifts it in the opposite direction.
The actual operating point is the intersection of cardiac-function and venous-return curves. Increased contractility can increase output while lowering right atrial pressure. Pump failure often reduces output and raises right atrial pressure. A very negative right atrial pressure eventually collapses susceptible great veins, limiting further return. More suction does not produce unlimited flow.

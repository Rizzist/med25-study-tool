## Answer key and provenance

### 1 · sums-retake-q001

Key: A — The number of calories present in each gram of food

Key provenance: Source mark with editorial review; not an official university key.

Existing answer note: Energy density is food energy divided by food mass, usually kcal/g. Nutrient density is a different idea.

Source: Original Q1; page 1.

DeBruyne, Pinna & Whitney, Nutrition & Diet Therapy, 9th ed. (2016), PDF pp. 29–30, 203.

### 2 · sums-retake-q002

Key: D — Elimination of food groups

Key provenance: Source mark with editorial review; not an official university key.

Existing answer note: Adequacy, balance and moderation support a varied diet. Routine elimination of entire food groups is not a general planning principle; specific allergies or diseases require individualized substitutions.

Source: Original Q2; page 1.

DeBruyne, Pinna & Whitney, Nutrition & Diet Therapy, 9th ed. (2016), PDF pp. 35–40.

### 3 · sums-retake-q003

Key: B — Steatorrhea and deficiency of fat-soluble vitamins

Key provenance: Source mark with editorial review; not an official university key.

Existing answer note: Unabsorbed fat causes fatty stools and impairs absorption of vitamins A, D, E and K. The stem already establishes fat malabsorption.

Source: Original Q3; page 1.

DeBruyne, Pinna & Whitney, Nutrition & Diet Therapy, 9th ed. (2016), PDF pp. 130–144, 210–224.

### 4 · sums-retake-q004

Key: B — Deficiency of calcium and vitamin D

Key provenance: Source mark with editorial review; not an official university key.

Existing answer note: Insufficient calcium and vitamin D compromise bone mineralization and are the best listed explanation. Diet history alone does not establish the cause of low bone density.

Source: Original Q4; page 1.

DeBruyne, Pinna & Whitney, Nutrition & Diet Therapy, 9th ed. (2016), PDF pp. 218–221, 254–257.

### 5 · sums-retake-q005

Key: C — Whole oats

Key provenance: Source mark with editorial review; not an official university key.

Existing answer note: Whole oats supply an intact food matrix and viscous beta-glucan fiber, slowing absorption compared with the listed refined or glucose-rich options.

Source: Original Q5; page 1.

DeBruyne, Pinna & Whitney, Nutrition & Diet Therapy, 9th ed. (2016), PDF pp. 69–74.

### 6 · sums-retake-q006

Key: C — Limiting added sugars and increasing fiber intake

Key provenance: Source mark with editorial review; not an official university key.

Existing answer note: Both replacing refined grains with whole grains and reducing added sugars while increasing fiber are sound changes. The source selects C, but its wording provides no fair basis to reject B; either is accepted.

Accepted alternatives: B, C

Source: Original Q6; page 1.

DeBruyne, Pinna & Whitney, Nutrition & Diet Therapy, 9th ed. (2016), PDF pp. 69–74, 576–585.

### 7 · sums-retake-q007

Key: A — Glycogen breakdown → Gluconeogenesis → Ketone body formation

Key provenance: Source mark with editorial review; not an official university key.

Existing answer note: Glycogenolysis initially supports glucose; gluconeogenesis becomes more important as glycogen falls, and ketone production rises with prolonged fasting. These processes overlap rather than switching on in isolation.

Source: Original Q7; page 2.

DeBruyne, Pinna & Whitney, Nutrition & Diet Therapy, 9th ed. (2016), PDF pp. 158–164.

### 8 · sums-retake-q008

Key: C — Deficiency of lactase enzyme leading to fermentation of undigested lactose in the large intestine

Key provenance: Source mark with editorial review; not an official university key.

Existing answer note: Reduced lactase leaves lactose unabsorbed. Osmotic water retention and colonic fermentation cause diarrhea, bloating and gas.

Source: Original Q8; page 2.

DeBruyne, Pinna & Whitney, Nutrition & Diet Therapy, 9th ed. (2016), PDF pp. 60–62.

### 9 · sums-retake-q009

Key: B — Replacing trans fats with polyunsaturated fats (PUFA)

Key provenance: Source mark with editorial review; not an official university key.

Existing answer note: Replacing industrial trans fat with unsaturated fat improves the lipid profile. The other choices do not provide an LDL-lowering substitution.

Source: Original Q9; page 2.

DeBruyne, Pinna & Whitney, Nutrition & Diet Therapy, 9th ed. (2016), PDF pp. 89–99.

### 10 · sums-retake-q010

Key: C — It is partially hydrogenated to increase shelf life

Key provenance: Source mark with editorial review; not an official university key.

Existing answer note: Partial hydrogenation historically generated industrial trans fatty acids while making oils more solid and shelf stable. Natural ruminant trans fats are an exception to inferring the process from trans fat alone.

Source: Original Q10; page 2.

DeBruyne, Pinna & Whitney, Nutrition & Diet Therapy, 9th ed. (2016), PDF pp. 86–89.

### 11 · sums-retake-q011

Key: C — Increases LDL and contributes to atherosclerosis

Key provenance: Source mark with editorial review; not an official university key.

Existing answer note: Many saturated fatty acids raise LDL relative to unsaturated-fat replacements. The clinical benefit of reducing saturated fat depends in part on what replaces it.

Source: Original Q11; page 2.

DeBruyne, Pinna & Whitney, Nutrition & Diet Therapy, 9th ed. (2016), PDF pp. 91–99.

### 12 · sums-retake-q012

Key: A — High energy content (9 kcal per gram)

Key provenance: Source mark with editorial review; not an official university key.

Existing answer note: Fat yields approximately 9 kcal/g versus 4 for carbohydrate or protein, and triacylglycerol is stored with little water. This makes it energy dense.

Source: Original Q12; page 2.

DeBruyne, Pinna & Whitney, Nutrition & Diet Therapy, 9th ed. (2016), PDF pp. 29–30.

### 13 · sums-retake-q013

Key: B — Lower

Source: Original Q13; page 3.

DeBruyne, Pinna & Whitney, Nutrition & Diet Therapy, 9th ed. (2016), PDF pp. 161–170, 394–395.

### 14 · sums-retake-q014
